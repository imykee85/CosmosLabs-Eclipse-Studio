# Sound, music, and voice

Use before selecting music, designing cues, directing narration, or mixing. Sound can carry narrative information, interiority, place, time, and point of view. It need not merely reinforce picture.

## Decide the listening experience

Choose what leads: dialogue, music, environmental sound, designed effects, silence, or a changing relationship among them. Describe what the listener should attend to and what changes through the sequence. Make space for intelligibility without assuming every other element must disappear beneath speech.

Consider four useful sound functions: atmosphere and place; a recognizable identity or recurring idea; selected causal events; and connection across edits. These are jobs, not four mandatory tracks. A performance can supply several; a sparse scene may need only one.

Distinguish sound within the scene from sound outside it, and objective space from a character's subjective hearing. Changes of perspective can be structural. Keep source truth clear when factual context matters; replacement effects or music must not imply a recorded event that did not occur.

Give consequential sound an opportunity to reveal information. We might hear a cause before seeing it, recognize a previously established source offscreen, or hear a place through someone's changing attention. Plan a listening moment and response that advance the scene, leaving enough picture and dialogue space for the sound to be understood. In nonfiction, select authentic listening and response from the material; do not fabricate a reaction or imply an event merely to make the sound effective. This applies [Randy Thom's practitioner guidance](https://www.filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm) as a scene-design option, not a universal formula.

## Build expectation musically

Make decisions about temporal organization, motif, harmony or tonal relationship, energy, and connection to picture. For tonal pulse-based music, useful mechanisms include:

- establish a pulse or recurring contour so variation can be perceived;
- change subdivision, register, voicing, articulation, syncopation, or layer density to alter pressure;
- suspend harmony, withhold bass, interrupt a pattern, or remove a layer to create space;
- repeat, truncate, sequence, augment, reharmonize, or change the timbre of an idea as its meaning develops;
- complete a phrase, return to stability, or deliberately withhold a cadence according to the ending.

A compact two-to-four-note motif can work well in a short, but identity can also reside in rhythm, texture, a longer phrase, a voice, or recurring natural sound. Do not require a tonal center, motif, or cadence of music whose language does not use them.

Choose tempo and meter from the performance, movement, phrase, and dramatic need. There is no universal emotional meaning in fast, slow, major, or minor. Rubato, mixed meter, silence, irregular phrases, and live timing are legitimate. Human feel can come from articulation, accent, phrasing, and systematic relationships between parts. Random independent jitter is a poor substitute for an intentional performance; exact quantization is not a universal ideal either.

With an existing recording, understand its phrase structure before editing it. Musical edits should preserve a convincing rhythmic, harmonic, and timbral relationship unless the break is intended. Lyrics can compete with dialogue or redefine an image; select and place them with that consequence in mind. When the established stack cannot supply the required musical performance, retain the musical intention and discuss the specific gap and an appropriate addition or workflow change with the user.

## Relate music to the edit selectively

Distinguish three relationships:

| Relationship | Possible treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Structural change: scene turn, chapter, revelation, departure | Cadence, downbeat, dropout, new texture, or deliberate counterpoint |
| Physical cause: contact, landing, opening, closure | Precisely placed attack or perceptually matched sound; start with sync within a frame when close contact demands it, then inspect picture and listen |
| Editorial flow: ordinary cut, angle change, image substitution | Carry the phrase, prelap, let a decay continue, or introduce no new sound |

Not every cut deserves a hit. A strong musical phrase can continue through cuts; picture can resist the beat to build expectation. A later return to alignment can then matter. Musical counterpoint is valid, but inspect whether it creates the intended ambiguity or an unsupported interpretation of a real person.

Before solving competition in the mix, decide how music and effects share pulse, sustained texture, low-frequency weight, and decisive impact. A source sound can supply rhythm while music provides texture, or the parts can exchange roles as the scene changes. Try removing or changing a competing part before ducking the whole score when the available material and music rights permit it. Preserve the recognizable cause of recorded action when it matters. These are arrangement options, not required layers or synchronization rules. The approach draws on [Christopher Nolan's account of composer–sound-designer collaboration](https://static.bafta.org/uploads_pre_202411/transcripts/baftaalifeinpictureschristophernolan.pdf#page=19) (BAFTA, 2017, p. 19); the role checklist is a practical synthesis.

## Give effects material and scale

Begin with the cause and perspective rather than a library category. Contact may need an attack, body, and resonance; friction may require filtered noise and duration; an opening can suggest a changing spectrum; a ripple can suggest damped resonance. Use recorded, performed, synthesized, or combined material according to the result.

Shape attack, release, decay, pitch movement, harmonic content, noise proportion, and modulation to fit weight and material. A heavy object is not made convincing merely by adding sub-bass. Match apparent distance, acoustic space, and dynamic scale to the shot or deliberately established point of view.

Align the click, body, and resonance of one event so they fuse. Separate independent events through onset, spectrum, timbre, level, or position. Avoid stacking equally prominent transients. One foreground event at a time is a useful clarity starting point; dense action may need a more complex hierarchy.

For a restrained design, primary peaks roughly 3–6 dB above supporting events and supporting events 6–10 dB above ambience can be a first audition. These are relative arrangement examples, not loudness standards. If the intended object or thought is unclear, simplify masking and competing attacks before raising the master.

Keep repetition meaningful. A single initial commitment and a destination sound may be stronger than a tick at every intermediate point. A graphic can remain silent. A recurring effect should develop with the event rather than announce the same emphasis indefinitely.

## Connect space, protect decay, use quiet

Prelap can prepare a place, speaker, or thought before picture arrives. Carried resonance or room tone can keep an outgoing space present. As short-form experiments, try prelaps around 120–300 ms or carries around 300–900 ms; longer bridges may be exactly right. Judge the phrase and dramatic relation rather than enforcing these ranges.

Audition overlap curves. Equal-power crossfades can help unrelated sustained material; correlated material may swell or cancel and needs different handling. Inspect starts, cuts, and endings for clicks, abrupt noise-floor changes, truncated reverbs, and unnatural recovery after ducking.

Distinguish absence of music, a sparse sound field, environmental quiet, a suspended musical layer, and literal digital silence. Each creates a different expectation. A documentary room may remain audible during a meaningful pause. A hard stop can be expressive when intentional; an accidentally amputated tail rarely is.

Low-level motion and sound can share a behavior without becoming another focal event. A slow resonance or small modulation may support a held image, but leave a still scene still when extra activity weakens it. Do not assign a cue to every visible animation.

## Protect and direct voice

For recorded speech, preserve meaning, connected phrasing, relevant breaths, laughter, hesitation, overlap, and room tone. Audition restoration for watery consonants, phase changes, gating, pumping, and lost character. Clean the problem that obstructs the work; do not erase meaningful place or performance for cosmetic silence.

For narration, cast for the piece: credibility, intimacy, age of delivery, energy, specificity, or other relevant qualities. Compare materially different readings when the choice is consequential, holding script and playback level stable. Do not infer a suitable performance from a preset name or gender stereotype.

Direct the connected phrase. Rhetorical lists, contrasts, and pairs often need continuing energy until a deliberate terminal cadence. Adjust meaning, punctuation, breath, emphasis, or available performance controls before attempting to manufacture expression in post. Audition the realized speech, not merely the text passed to a tool. Avoid clause stitching that sounds segmented or changes the thought.

With synthetic narration, retain the approved wording, performance text or directions, selected audio, and relevant voice/model provenance. Freeze the selected performance for the edit; a regenerated take is a new take to hear. Do not infer consent, endorsement, or distribution rights from a voice label. Human and synthetic recordings both require appropriate authorized use.

## Mix for the intended experience

Retain dialogue, music, effects, and ambience separation where revisions or handoff need it. Establish the hierarchy before loudness processing. Carve spectral or temporal space for consonants and important details; use phrase-level rides or controlled ducking when warranted. Listen to attack and recovery so the bed does not audibly breathe for the wrong reason. A compressor's numbers do not approve its behavior.

For conventional stereo/mobile delivery, keep essential dialogue and other necessary information sufficiently central to survive mono playback. Place width where it supports space and movement. Do not automatically narrow intentional stereo recordings or transfer this stereo practice to immersive formats without considering their monitoring and delivery requirements.

Check small-speaker translation: useful harmonics can preserve the identity of low notes without simply raising sub-bass. A 180 Hz high-pass audition can expose bass dependence, but is only a rough proxy for a phone, not a substitute for listening on the intended device. Compare mono for disappearing elements and phase problems. Spectrograms, channel balance, correlation, and peaks locate issues; context and listening decide their significance.

Use the destination's current loudness and peak requirements where specified. In their absence, choose a documented review target appropriate to the program; approximately -18 LUFS integrated and -1 dBTP can be an initial short-web audition, not a universal delivery mandate. Very short pieces can make loudness-range statistics misleading. Preserve intended dynamics, use gain before unnecessary limiting, and hear what peak control does to attacks and decays.

Measure and audition the decoded delivery, including sync, onset, tail, intelligibility, distortion, stereo/mono behavior, and codec effects. Test quieter playback as well as normal level. Accessibility for meaningful speech and sound, and the appropriate silent-viewing version, are addressed in [Finishing and review](finishing-and-review.md).
