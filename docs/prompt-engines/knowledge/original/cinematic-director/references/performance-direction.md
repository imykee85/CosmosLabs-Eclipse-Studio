# Directing human performance

Use this guide when acting, dialogue, listening, emotional restraint, physical effort, or interactions between people carry the shot. These are directing heuristics and testable prompt hypotheses, not guarantees of human-looking output. Do not solve a robotic performance by adding an inventory of blinks, twitches, facial muscles, or the words “fully human.”

## Give the actor something to do

Establish only the facts that change visible behavior:

- **Objective:** what does this person want from this moment or another person?
- **Obstacle:** what resists them—another person's response, a physical load, lack of time, fear of being noticed?
- **Tactic:** what do they try—reassure, distract, conceal, test, persuade, rush, hold back?
- **Attention:** what are they watching or listening for, and what event changes that focus?
- **Physical state:** which relevant constraint affects the attempt—fatigue, injury, cold, balance, wet clothing, a carried object?

These are planning tools, not mandatory headings or a character biography. Convert the useful parts into a few observable actions. “She wants him to stay but avoids asking directly” can become leaving his coat within reach while prolonging a routine question. Preserve the user's intended action rather than inventing a new subplot to demonstrate psychology.

## Connect behavior to events

Build a readable causal chain: **opening activity → stimulus → response or attempted concealment → consequence → continuing state**. Give the person something they were already doing, where appropriate, so the reaction interrupts a life rather than beginning a demonstration for the camera.

Allow channels to overlap: an answer can start before a hand finishes setting down a cup; a foot can correct balance while attention stays on a partner. Do not make every gesture a separate stop-start instruction. Use approximate beat windows to leave room for noticing, effort, and response; reserve precise timing for story-critical cues.

Do not make “eyes first, head second, body third” a universal rule. A sudden sound may turn the head and torso together; a careful sideways check may move only the gaze. Choose coordination from the stimulus and physical situation. A small delay is useful when it expresses processing, not because all reactions need a prescribed delay.

## Let effort and recovery remain visible

For meaningful physical action, consider **preparation → loading/contact → resistance → adjustment → follow-through → recovery**. Include the stages visible at this scale, not all six as compulsory beats.

A person lifting a heavy case changes grip and loads their legs; the case initially resists; their balance adjusts as it clears the floor. When they stop, their body and the hanging load need not settle at the same instant. Describe contact, support, and the effect of mass, rather than requesting an exact physical simulation.

Natural movement has continuity, but its speed is not uniform. It can brake, stiffen, hesitate, or become abrupt when motivated. “Fluid” must not erase weight, urgency, fatigue, or pain. Let exertion affect breath and subsequent action; do not reset a runner to calm breathing at the cut. Avoid a fixed breathing rhythm or exaggerated chest movement in every scene.

## Individualize without adding noise

Use asymmetry when it has a reason: favoring an injured side, protecting a bag, leaning toward a quiet speaker, or supporting weight on the available hand. Preserve handedness and existing physical constraints. Do not prescribe permanent facial asymmetry, random imbalance, or compulsive movement as shorthand for realism.

In an ensemble, people react according to what they perceive, their objective, and their position. One person may notice the door alarm first; another keeps an eye on the bag; a third is still catching up. Let timing differ without inventing unrelated actions or violating a deliberate synchronized performance.

Stillness is an active option. Someone can hold position while listening for footsteps or deciding whether to answer. Name that attention and the visible restraint if needed; do not fill every pause with blinking, swallowing, lip biting, head tilts, or camera shake.

## Match nuance to the image

- **Wide or fast action:** prioritize route, silhouette, pace, balance, gesture, spacing, and prop handling. Do not demand readable pores or tiny eyelid changes.
- **Medium:** use posture, hands, gaze targets, interrupted tasks, and the relationship between speakers.
- **Close-up with a readable hold:** choose a small, motivated cue—a smile briefly held after the listener stops smiling, a mouth preparing an answer that is withheld, or a grip easing as reassurance lands.

Interpret a request for “micro-expressions” as restrained observable performance. Do not claim a facial movement reliably reveals a hidden emotion, deception, or mental state. Do not schedule involuntary expressions to the frame or animate every facial feature. One useful cue can carry more than a catalogue. Keep neutral or ambiguous expressions when the story calls for them.

## Direct speech and listening together

Preserve exact dialogue and speaker assignment. Distinguish the words from the intention behind them: reassurance may be tentative, a polite answer may try to end the exchange. Give concise delivery direction grounded in the situation rather than stacking emotional adjectives.

Specify what the listener attends to and whether the line changes their behavior. They may continue a task, stop midway, or withhold a response; do not require a nod after every sentence. Leave time for speech, breath, pauses, and reactions. Do not add filler words, overlap, extra speech, or rewritten lines without authorization. A listener must not mouth another speaker's words.

## Use performance references deliberately

When an actual host and mode support a motion/performance reference, inspect the supplied clip and identify the useful timing, weight, posture, gesture, or interaction. State which qualities to borrow and which identity, wardrobe, setting, camera, or audio to retain from other sources. Verify upload role and syntax; an identity image is not automatically a motion guide. Do not invent support, imply unseen media were inspected, or promise exact choreography or lip sync from a reference.

## Three small rewrites

**Vague:** “She looks nervous, blinking naturally, with realistic micro-expressions.”

**Directed:** “While folding the receipt, she hears her name from the doorway. The fold stops unfinished. She keeps looking at the paper for a beat, then answers the person without unfolding it.”

**Mechanical:** “He smoothly picks up the heavy case, turns, and walks.”

**Directed:** “He takes the handle and starts the lift; the case barely shifts. He widens his stance, brings it close to his leg, and lifts again. Once upright, he adjusts his grip before taking the first short step.”

**Overacted:** “She says ‘I'm fine’ with trembling lips, darting eyes, a twitch, and a sad smile.”

**Directed:** “She wants to end the question. Still aligning the cups, she says exactly, ‘I'm fine.’ When her partner remains silent, she places the last cup more carefully and leaves her hand resting beside it.”

These examples illustrate choices, not interchangeable recipes. Judge the actual take before changing direction; preserve successful behavior and revise one likely cause first.

## Basis and limits

The [Neighborhood Playhouse's description of Meisner training](https://www.neighborhoodplayhouse.org/home) emphasizes attention on another person, listening, preparation, and truthful response under imaginary circumstances (checked 30 October 2026). Adapting these acting principles into stimulus-and-response prompt direction is a working hypothesis, not evidence that Seedance implements an acting technique. The examples above are original and have not been validated by generated takes.
