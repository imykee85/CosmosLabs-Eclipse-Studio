# Direction grammar

These are synthesized techniques, not copied Higgsfield prompts or mandatory headings. Keep the final prompt proportional to the shot. Use plain words a filmmaker can act on.

## Make the shot legible

Translate the desired feeling into observable change. “She is nervous” is incomplete direction; a fingertip stops tapping when footsteps approach, her gaze reaches the doorway before her head turns, and she keeps her answer measured. Select one or two telling actions rather than animating every feature.

Establish the few spatial facts needed to avoid ambiguity:

- Camera's position and height, framing, foreground and background.
- Where the subject starts, faces and moves; where the action ends.
- What is visible versus deliberately off-screen.
- Relevant eyeline target and which side of the action axis each setup occupies.
- A prop's owner, hand, orientation and state before and after contact.

Left/right must have a reference system. Use “screen-left” for composition and “her right hand” for anatomy. Do not silently mirror a vehicle, injury, outfit detail or travel direction. Numeric coordinates can help a dense blocking problem, but natural near/far and left/right relationships are usually more portable. Percentages and exact timing are direction targets, not guaranteed pixel/frame locks.

## Separate reference responsibilities

Write a scope for each relevant input: **use for X; do not inherit Y**. For example, identity portrait → face/hair, outfit image → garments only, location plate → architecture/materials, storyboard → composition, motion clip → trajectory/timing. A location reference need not force the source camera angle. A portrait need not force a static facial expression. A starting image is different: the generated action must plausibly begin from its visible arrangement.

Do not override a visible image with incompatible instructions and expect wording to repair it. If a required character, prop or camera position cannot be reached from the approved first frame, suggest an updated first frame or a separate setup. Mark missing reference slots honestly. Assets may guide identity, but “100% match” cannot guarantee it.

## Time actions instead of stacking adjectives

A useful single-shot structure is:

**Opening state → trigger → physical action → reaction → final hold.**

Choose approximate beats only when they clarify pacing. For six seconds, two or three beats may be enough. Account for travel distance, acceleration, hand contact, dialogue and reaction time. If ten events compete for five seconds, remove nonessential events or split the scene.

For a sequence, name every shot and cut explicitly, then give each setup a single camera behavior. List intervals whose total equals the requested generation length. Re-establish its background and screen direction when changing angles. Carry the final prop/pose state from one shot into the next. A half-second wide reference anchor found in some production prompts is one technique, not a requirement for every film.

Hard cuts, dissolves, flash transitions and match cuts do different jobs. Use the transition the user requests or the story warrants. A single uninterrupted take must not contain hidden edits, lens switches or sudden viewpoint changes. Never describe a multi-shot montage as an unbroken shot.

## Direct a camera with a reason to move

Choose shot size and perspective first, then lens appearance and movement. The camera's path should reveal information, accompany action, create intimacy or hold tension. Give it an end condition: “tracks alongside at walking speed, then settles when she stops.” Avoid “cinematic dynamic camera” without geometry.

Use one dominant motion per setup. A dolly, arc and handheld texture can coexist only when the trajectory and scale are physically clear; do not stack a push-in, crane, orbit and whip-pan into one short beat. Keep true camera motion separate from a subject moving toward the camera or an editorial punch-in.

Lens family, focal-length appearance, subject distance and depth of field should agree. Do not copy exact degree-to-millimetre conversions without the sensor/gate and squeeze assumptions. Do not prescribe shallow focus while requiring widely separated objects to be equally sharp. State a motivated focus transfer only when it matters; otherwise maintain the focal priority.

Anamorphic can mean gentle edge character, oval defocused highlights, restrained halation and a motivated horizontal flare. It does not require heavy distortion, permanent blue streaks, fake letterboxing or a particular output ratio. Keep faces and architecture readable. An image may have restrained grain without losing fine texture; “restrained grain” and “no grain” are alternatives, not simultaneous instructions.

## Let physical causes create realism

Make visible contact specific: a fingertip drags against rough timber; skin compresses slightly; a loose fibre catches and releases; the hand slows with friction. A foot first loads the ground, the surface gives, then the body compensates. A vehicle brakes, the front suspension dips, loose contents settle. Describe a plausible response rather than promising a physics simulation.

Match the scale. Wide shots benefit from silhouette, weight transfer and environmental interaction. Close shots can resolve pressure, moisture, fabric weave or a small involuntary reaction. Do not request visible pores in a distant full-body wide.

Backgrounds can remain alive without competing with the story: independent pedestrian paths, intermittent foliage movement, a curtain responding to a breeze, distant traffic, small reflections moving as objects pass. Give motion a cause and varied timing. Avoid adding weather, steam, fog, extras or camera shake by default. Quiet rooms and locked cameras can feel real too.

Avoid mechanical performance recipes. Natural breathing, occasional blinks and small posture shifts should follow the scene's state; do not schedule everyone to blink together or prescribe a constant heartbeat-like gesture. Emotional reactions arrive through behavior and timing, not a list of facial adjectives.

## Motivate light and sound

Specify the source, its world-space position, softness, color relationship, falloff and exposure priority. A warm desk lamp can model one face while a cool screen affects the opposite side; changing the camera angle does not move the lamp. Describe reflectance honestly: polished metal, sweaty skin and glass can shine. Do not flatten all materials into matte surfaces.

Choose palette relationships to support the scene. A dominant/secondary/accent palette is useful; exact 60/30/10 numbers are not a model requirement. Warm light is appropriate to a warm practical or low sun, not compulsory in a cold fluorescent scene. Preserve existing light when continuing from a frame; avoid adding unmotivated beauty fill or fog.

Give sounds a visible cause: the weight of a footfall, paper drag, a chain settling, a chair creak, distant traffic. Distinguish diegetic sound, score, voiceover and dialogue. If dialogue is requested, assign the exact words to one named speaker, specify delivery succinctly, and direct the listener. Leave pauses and reaction time. A silent listener should not mouth the other actor's words. Do not invent lines or replace exact text to fit an undersized runtime; flag the conflict.

For screens, precise signs and composited text, state whether the image should contain the final content or a stable plate to replace later. Do not direct readable moving content and a motionless replacement marker simultaneously.

## Remove contradictions before adding emphasis

Create one canonical version of the timing, pose, dialogue and ending. Compare every repetition with it. Remove stale variants rather than putting all of them in a concluding lock block. A brief final continuity list is useful; repeating the entire action several times creates more chances for disagreement.

Source examples included incompatible lens-family phrases, seated-versus-lying states, silent-versus-voiceover instructions and conflicting beat schedules. Their inclusion in a professional project does not prove they helped. Preserve explicit reference roles, staging and cause/effect; improve the consistency.

Keep scene-writing and generation controls separate. A production prompt may say “8K” while the asset metadata says 4K. Copying that phrase will not select 8K. Read the exact current provider settings, then verify an actual output before claiming its quality or dimensions.
