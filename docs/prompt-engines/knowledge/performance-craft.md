PERFORMANCE CRAFT

Eclipse notes written from the owner's "Performance Bible" skill (the original files are kept unchanged in docs/prompt-engines/knowledge/original/performance-bible/). Use it whenever a scene has people who act: dialogue, a listener, a tense or emotional moment, two people in a place, or when the person says a character looks stiff, fake or "AI". It turns a scene idea into characters with real motives and writes their behavior as plain performance directions you can put inside any video prompt (Seedance, Kling, Wan and the rest), a shot list, or notes for an actor. It directs the PEOPLE. Camera, lighting, reference pictures and model settings are decided elsewhere (Cinema Craft, the Seedance notes, the screen's own controls).

CORE RULE: THE CAMERA SEES VERBS, NOT PSYCHOLOGY
Every inner state must end as a physical action, a breath, an eyeline, a timing choice or a vocal quality. Words like "nervous", "insecure" or "confident" never appear in the final directions. "Drums two fingers against the mug" is a direction; "seems anxious" is not.

HOW TO WORK (do not stop to ask questions; make choices, say them, let the person swap anything)
1. READ THE SETUP: the place, how many people, any relationship or tension. If none is given, invent a relationship and a subtext conflict: one person wants something the other is withholding. If they say "random", pick contrasting archetypes and origins at random.
2. BUILD EACH CHARACTER from an origin (Layer 1) and an archetype (Layer 2). Unless told otherwise make two people contrast: different archetype, different movement tempo, different origin. If the person gave only a few traits, list which traits were GIVEN and which you INFERRED.
3. SET THE SCENE ENGINE (Layer 4): each person's objective, their secret, who holds status at the start and at the end. Status flips at least once, on a visible physical event.
4. WRITE 3 TO 6 BEATS. For each: the hidden state underneath, the TRIGGER (what the other person just said or did), each person's tells (Layer 3 and the state table in section 4 below), chosen for what the audience should READ, not only what the character feels. Every beat gives the LISTENER a behavior too.
5. WRITE THE PERFORMANCE DIRECTIONS (Layer 5).
6. HAND OVER the directions so they can drop straight into a prompt (see YOUR DELIVERY RULES).

LAYER 1: ORIGINS GIVE HABITS (tendencies, never rules; blend with the archetype, avoid caricature)
- Loud, crowded household: big, fast, interrupting gestures; leans in; talks over others; eats fast, reaches across the table.
- Quiet, strict household: minimal gestures, hands visible and still; keeps distance; soft voice, waits for gaps, apologizes before speaking; upright, napkin in lap.
- Volatile household: small gestures close to the body; back to the wall facing the door; measured voice that tracks the other person's tone; scans the room, startles at sudden noise, placating half-smiles.
- Working-class or manual-labor: hands used like tools; sits wide; direct, few words; forearms on the table.
- Affluent or old money: economical, unhurried; takes space without effort; slow voice that doesn't fill silence; small gestures carry weight.
- Rural or small town: slow, nods a lot; relaxed distance; long pauses; leans on elbows, unhurried.
- Big city: sharp punctuating gestures; efficient close distance; fast overlapping speech; eyes track the surroundings, impatient foot.
- High-contact cultures (Mediterranean, Latin American, Middle Eastern): wide rhythmic gestures from the shoulder; close, touches the other's arm; expressive pitch; animated face.
- Restraint-valued cultures (East Asian, Nordic): small gestures from the wrist; more distance; even pitch, comfortable with silence; less sustained eye contact or long calm silences.
- US South: warm softening rituals; polite before direct; stretched vowels; smiles while disagreeing.
- Military or ex-athlete: minimal, precise; squared to the table; clipped speech; very still hands.
- DIALECT: name a specific region, give one or two audible features, plus pace, volume and pitch range.

LAYER 2: TEN ARCHETYPES (fear, defense, baseline body, under pressure, tell when hiding)
1. The Pleaser (anxious, agreeable): fear abandonment, defense appeasing. Shallow high-chest breath, leans forward with rounded shoulders, searches the other's face, fidgets with a napkin or sleeve. Under pressure over-explains, laughs at the wrong moments, over-mirrors. Hiding: a smile that doesn't reach the eyes, swallows before speaking.
2. The Fortress (avoidant): fear being engulfed or needed, defense withdrawal. Slow controlled breath, leans back with an arm along the booth, steady but disengaged gaze often out the window, hands still around the cup. Under pressure gets quieter, checks the phone, sets the jaw. Hiding: lips press thin, slow blinks.
3. The Performer (high extraversion, narcissistic edge): fear being ordinary or unseen, defense charm. Full diaphragm breath, open posture that takes space, long eye contact while checking who is watching, big illustrative gestures. Under pressure gets louder, jokes, touches the other's arm. Hiding: tells a long story, holds eye contact a beat too long.
4. The Survivor (hypervigilant, volatile upbringing): fear danger and being caught off guard, defense watchfulness and control. Held or interrupted breath, back to the wall facing the door, coiled, scans the room every few seconds, hands under the table or tight to the body. Under pressure freezes, then turns sharp. Hiding: goes perfectly still.
5. The Fixer (conscientious, control-oriented): fear chaos or failure, defense organizing. Steady breath with frustrated sighs, upright and aligned, direct purposeful gaze, arranges objects. Under pressure lists solutions, answers emotion with logistics. Hiding: keeps the hands busy.
6. The Ghost (depressive, low energy): fear being a burden, defense invisibility. Slow breath with heavy exhales, collapsed posture, chin down, looks down or away with long blank stares, limp hands. Under pressure delayed answers, one-word replies. Hiding: flat face, nods.
7. The Hustler (open, street-smart): fear being trapped, defense humor and deflection. Easy breath, loose posture angled toward the exit, quick eyes that read people, hands always moving (coin, lighter, sugar packet). Under pressure changes the subject, smiles wider. Hiding: touches nose or mouth, speeds up.
8. The Judge (conscientious, low agreeableness): fear being wrong or looking foolish, defense criticism. Tight breath, exhales through the nose, rigid posture, arms crossed or fingertips steepled, evaluating gaze with raised brows, precise pointing hands. Under pressure colder and more formal. Hiding: purses lips, glances away, then looks back harder.
9. The Dreamer (open, high neuroticism): fear being unloved or meaningless, defense idealization. Uneven breath with sighs, soft posture, chin on hand, gazes out the window then turns suddenly intense, touches own face or hair, traces the cup rim. Under pressure eyes well up, overstates poetically. Hiding: stares into the distance.
10. The Steady One (secure): little fear shows, defense direct communication. Slow belly breath, relaxed and upright, warm eye contact with natural breaks, calm open palms. Under pressure pauses, then names the problem aloud. Hiding: rarely; a slight delay before answering.
Most drama needs at least one insecure archetype; the Steady One works best as a contrast. KNOWN GAP: there is no secure extravert ("confident and extroverted" falls into the Performer and picks up its fear of being ordinary and narcissistic edge): use the Performer's baseline body with the Steady One's attachment, and say so.

LAYER 3: TELLS BY EMOTION (modulate by archetype: expressive types amplify, restrained types suppress, and suppressed emotion leaks through hands, feet, breath and swallowing)
LEAKAGE ORDER: the face is the most controlled, the hands next, the legs and feet the least. For subtle scenes put the truth under the table: a bouncing knee, feet pointed at the exit, hands gripping the seat.
- Anxiety: shallow breath, swallowing, licking lips or teeth, touching the neck, bouncing knee, picking cuticles, checking the phone.
- Shame: eyes down, touches the face, shrinks, turns a shoulder away, voice drops.
- Suppressed anger: jaw clench, nostril flare, sudden stillness, palm pressed flat on the table, slow blink, short exhale. Expressed anger: leans in, points, louder, chopping hand gestures.
- Grief: heavy exhale, unfocused eyes, slow movement, holds the cup without drinking.
- Attraction or affection: leans in, mirrors the other, lingering eye contact, touches own neck or hair, feet turned toward them.
- Contempt: one-sided lip raise, eye roll, leans back, looks away while the other talks.
- Deception: too still or more self-touching, delayed answers, a lopsided smile, more detail than the question needs.
- Fear: freezes, eyes widen briefly, holds breath, pulls hands in.
- Relief: long exhale, shoulders drop, a short laugh, sits back.
Extra channels worth naming: brows up with a head tilt (surprise, skepticism, interest); lips pressed or pulled in (uncertainty, holding back); a sigh with the head tipped back (frustration); a slow nod with the head tilted (empathy).

LAYER 4: SCENE DYNAMICS (two people)
- OBJECTIVE: a verb aimed at the other person ("to get her to admit she's leaving", "to make him laugh so he won't ask"). OBSTACLE: the other person's objective. TACTICS change by beat: charm, guilt, threaten, plead, deflect, withdraw.
- STATUS: high status shows as stillness, taking time, holding space, not filling silence; low status as more movement, filling silence, shrinking. Flip it at least once and make the flip a prop or body event the audience cannot miss (a receipt slid across the table, a step back, a turn away); a twitch or a single nod is easy to lose.
- MIRRORING reads as connection; mismatched rhythm reads as conflict.
- RHYTHM REGISTER, one per scene, stated: NATURALISTIC (answers start about 0.4 s after the other stops, a third of answers have a gap over 1 s, about a third of turn switches overlap) or HEIGHTENED (the acted register: overlap on about half the switches, answers landing almost immediately). A gap over 2 s, or a gap filled with a visible behavior, is what makes a pause mean something.
- THE LISTENER IS HALF THE SCENE: half of all notable moments happen while someone is listening. Give the listener a nonverbal reaction every 3 to 5 seconds of the partner's speech. Spoken "yeah" or "mm-hmm" are rare (about 1 to 2 a minute), use them sparingly.
- INCLUDE ONE SILENT BEAT where nobody speaks and the behavior is the scene: give it a length ("about four seconds") and something visible to watch.
- BLOCKING: decide who faces the door, how far each leans, which props they use (coffee, menu, fries, napkin, phone, the check).

SECTION 4: MEASURED HUMAN BEHAVIOR (from an analysis of a large public study of real two-person conversations; use as a guide, not law)
SIX RULES
1. Every nuance is a reaction: write the trigger first ("When she says ___, he ___"). About 57% of observers' reasons name something the partner just did or said (65% for listening moments).
2. A nuance lasts about 3 seconds (most 1 to 6) and uses one or two body channels: budget one nuance per 3-second window per character.
3. Half of notable moments (50%) happen while the person is LISTENING, 42% while speaking. Listeners nod, tilt the head, move lips and mouth, raise brows. Speakers gesture with their hands.
4. Eyebrows are the most recorded single feature (20% of moments, 48% of surprise, 40% of skepticism); head tilt is close behind (17%). Name them in directions.
5. Real conversation has gaps; acted conversation overlaps. Ordinary people start a median 0.4 s after the partner stops and a third of gaps are over 1 s; actors start a median 0.07 s after and overlap on 45% of switches.
6. Write for what the audience READS, not what the character feels: a person's own account of a moment and the observer's read matched only 29% of the time.
TIMING NUMBERS: ordinary talk: median gap 0.40 s, middle half from 0.19 s of overlap to 1.35 s of gap, 31% of switches overlap, 32% of gaps over 1 s, 15% over 2 s, median turn 4.3 s (about 11 words). Acted talk: median gap 0.07 s, 45% overlap, 19% of gaps over 1 s, median turn 3.3 s. Pauses inside a turn: about 0.42 s, one in six over 1 s: these are where speakers look away, tilt the head or press the lips, so one written-in mid-line pause reads more natural than a clean delivery. A one-second silence before an answer is NORMAL, not dramatic. Friends and strangers showed almost no timing difference, so do not set rhythm by relationship alone.
BACKCHANNELS (spoken "I'm listening"): about 1.3 a minute of partner speech in ordinary talk, 2.4 for actors; half land while the partner is still talking; the words are "yeah" first, then "okay", "right", "yes", "mm-hmm". In a 10 to 15 second scene a verbal one is optional, at most one: nonverbal listening carries the scene.
WHAT OBSERVERS SEE (share of notable moments): eyebrows 20%, head tilt 17%, lips and mouth 17%, hand gesture 16% (speakers 24%), smile 13%, nod 11% (listeners 15%), lean 11%, eyes (widen, close, squint, blink) 10%, gaze shift 9%, laugh 6%, self-touch 5%, head shake 5%, sigh or visible breath 4%, shrug 3%, arms crossed 1%. Listeners show lips 24%, head tilt 19%, nod 15%. About a quarter of moments are a short two-step sequence ("nodded, then shook the head"); three or more steps in 3 seconds is rare. Mixed signals (a smile with a frown) and freezes are rare: use them as deliberate choices.
STATE TO BEHAVIOR (phrasings you can use):
- Confident or assertive (mostly while speaking): hand gestures, brows up, head tilt, nods. Phrasings: holds both palms up toward the partner while explaining; clasped hands open and move forward; looks straight at the partner, brows lifted.
- Engaged or interested (mostly listening): brows, mouth, nods, head tilt, smile. Small nods while the other talks; head tilted, brows lifted; leans in slightly.
- Empathetic or agreeing: nod most of all (38%), brows, hands. Nods slowly, head tilted; presses lips together; open palms with a small shrug.
- Happy: smile (34%), brows, nods. Smiles and nods; brows lift with the smile.
- Amused: laugh (49%), smile, head tilt, lean. Laughs and leans back; covers the mouth with a hand while laughing; a small laugh, then looks down.
- Surprised (mostly listening): brows (48%), mouth, eyes, head pulls back. Eyebrows shoot up, mouth opens; head pulls back a few centimeters; hand goes to the cheek or mouth.
- Skeptical: brows (40%) and head tilt (31%), shrug. One brow up, head tilted; shrugs with arms out; side glance with a half-smile.
- Uncertain: mouth, brows, head tilt, gaze away. Pulls the lips in or purses them; glances to the side; hand to the mouth or hair; squeezes own hands.
- Thinking: head tilt, lips, nod. Looks down and bites the lip; squints, eyes to the side; lips make an "O".
- Uncomfortable or nervous: head tilt, brows, gaze away, a brief tight smile. Glances away, brows up; scratches the chin, blinks a few times; shifts weight.
- Frustrated: hands, brows, mouth, sigh. Sighs and tips the head back, eyes close; bites the lip; palm out toward the partner; rubs the nose or eye.
- Sad or disappointed: head tilt, lips, gaze down. Lowers the face and looks down; puffs the cheeks and exhales; lips pressed tight; hand over the face.
PERSONALITY: the Big Five did NOT predict how much someone talks, how long their turns are, or how often they backchannel (every correlation within plus or minus 0.12). Extraversion shows up in CHANNELS: more hand gestures (22% vs 16% of notable moments), eyebrow movement (17% vs 6%), eye contact (7% vs 1%) and leaning in (12% vs 8%). So play an extravert through gesture, brows, eye contact and lean, NOT extra lines or volume. Laughter is not an extravert marker: quieter people laughed in more of their moments (a release or deflection works for any type). Trust the archetype over an actor's own traits.

LAYER 5: WRITING PERFORMANCE DIRECTIONS (any model)
These are writing practices, not claims about how a particular video model responds: test and keep what reads.
- VERB + BODY PART + OBJECT: "Drums two fingers against the mug."
- TRIGGER FIRST, THEN THE RESPONSE: "When she says ___, he ___."
- ABOUT ONE NUANCE PER 3 SECONDS PER CHARACTER, one or two channels, at most a two-step sequence ("raises his eyebrows, then tilts his head").
- THE LISTENER ALWAYS DOES SOMETHING (a nod, brows up with a head tilt, lips pressed, a brief smile, or an ongoing task) and the directions say the listener is not speaking while the other talks.
- MATCH DETAIL TO FRAME SIZE: close shot: brows, lips, eyes, breath, swallowing. Medium: hands, head, posture, props. Wide: posture, spacing, gait, big gestures.
- NAME EVERY EYELINE: at the partner, at the object, away and down, out the window.
- DIALOGUE: exact words in quotes, who says them, and the delivery in plain words (pace, volume, pitch, one or two accent features). Say how the answer lands: "after a short beat", "a full two seconds pass", "she starts before he finishes".
- STATUS FLIPS AND SILENT BEATS GET A VISIBLE EVENT: a prop handed over, a step back, a turn away, a timed stillness.
- KEEP OUT: psychology adjectives, backstory, anything the audience cannot see.
- Small channels carry the nuance: brows and lips are where real nuance lives. Give each nuance a clear trigger and a few seconds of its own so it has room to read.

OUTPUT FORMAT (in this order)
1. CAST, compact, per character: name and age; look and wardrobe; archetype; origin; attachment; core fear; want in this scene; secret; baseline body; voice (and which traits were given vs inferred when the person gave only a few).
2. SCENE ENGINE: relationship; subtext; rhythm register (naturalistic or heightened); status at open to status at close, with the physical event that flips it.
3. BEATS: beat number; trigger; what is happening underneath (one line); A's behavior; B's behavior (the listener always gets one).
4. PERFORMANCE DIRECTIONS: one numbered block per shot or moment, plain behavior paragraphs ready to drop into a prompt. No model syntax, camera settings or technical parameters unless asked.

WORKED EXAMPLE (setup: two characters in a diner booth at night)
Cast: Marcus, 38: broad, worn work jacket, two-day stubble. The Survivor, raised in South Philly in a volatile household. Fear: being blindsided. Wants his sister to come to their father's funeral. Secret: he already paid for it. Sits facing the door with his hands under the table, scanning the entrance. Fast, low Philly voice that clips word endings. Dani, 31: camel coat she keeps on, hair pulled back tight. The Fortress, left home at 18, lives in Seattle. Fear: being pulled back in. Wants to leave without promising anything. Secret: she already booked a flight. Leans back with an arm along the booth, watches the rain on the window. Even, quiet voice with long pauses.
Scene engine: estranged siblings; underneath, the real question is who raised whom. Status opens with Dani (calm, he needs something) and flips to Marcus when he slides the funeral-home receipt across the table.
Direction 1: Late-night diner booth, rain on the window. A broad man in a worn work jacket sits facing the door with both hands under the table edge, glancing past her shoulder at the entrance every few seconds. Across from him a woman in a camel coat leans back against the booth with one arm along its top, slowly stirring coffee she doesn't drink, her eyes on the window. Neither speaks for about four seconds. When the bell over the door rings, his eyes go to it before his head turns; she doesn't look up.

VIEWING CHECKLIST (use it when the person shares a take, or when you check your own directions)
- Does each reaction have a visible trigger from the other person?
- Does the listener do something every 3 to 5 seconds (nod, brows, tilt, lips) without mouthing the speaker's words?
- Are gaps between lines plausible (mostly 0.2 to 1.5 s), or do the lines run together?
- Did the eyebrows and head move at all, or is the face a mask while only the hands move?
- Is any expression held well past 6 seconds (a frozen smile)?
- For an "extroverted" character: is it carried by gestures, eyes and lean rather than by talking more?
Compare fairly: change one direction at a time and judge two or more takes per version; one good or bad take proves nothing. When the same behavior keeps failing to read, change HOW it is directed (a bigger trigger, a longer window, a different channel).

LIMITS (say them plainly when they matter)
These numbers describe two people face to face, standing or sitting; they do not cover groups of three or more, walking, or outdoor locations. Most annotated examples came from actors, so the ordinary-talk numbers are the guide for real-feeling scenes and the acted numbers for heightened ones. They describe how PEOPLE behave, not how a video model reproduces it: you cannot see the result, so ask what read on screen and adjust. You cannot hear or watch a video's performance unless it is attached and you watch it as frames (no sound).

HOW THIS MEETS ECLIPSE
- A reply that carries a scene (dialogue or a short moment) becomes the person's video prompt: put the performance directions in the prompt's action beats (with timestamps when the model reads them, such as Seedance 2.5), keep each beat to about 3 seconds and one or two channels, and keep dialogue in quotes. For a speaking character that must keep one voice across shots, also offer Character Voice Lock.
- Keep the whole prompt inside the model's length and clip limits (Seedance 2.5 clips are 4 to 30 seconds): a 10 to 15 second two-person scene fits 3 to 5 beats.
- Picture references: a face the model must keep comes from the character's reference image (image 1, image 2...); describe the PERFORMANCE in words and never describe a real person's face from a picture.

YOUR DELIVERY RULES (Atlas, Performance Craft)
- Make the choices yourself and state them in the Cast section; the person can swap anything. Do not ask clarifying questions first.
- Show Cast, Scene engine and Beats compactly, then hand the finished performance directions over through suggest_prompt (one prompt per reply; for several shots, the first shot's prompt through suggest_prompt and the rest in code blocks) so they fill the prompt box. Keep to the prompt limit of the model in the box.
- If the box already holds a prompt, improve it: add triggers, a listener behavior, a silent beat or a status flip rather than rewriting its look.
- After a take, use the viewing checklist, ask what read and what did not, and change one direction at a time.
