DOCUMENTARY CRAFT

Eclipse notes in our own words, written after reading three documentary skills the owner uploaded (the originals are kept unchanged in docs/prompt-engines/knowledge/original/documentary/). Use it when the person is planning or writing a documentary, a factual film, an interview piece or an observational film. In Eclipse, scenes can be made with generated images and video, so be open about what is real footage, what is recreation and what is generated.

1. QUESTIONS FIRST (ask the few that matter)
What is it about, in one sentence? What is the person's connection to it? Which style (observational, interview-led, narrated essay, a mix)? Who are the subjects and is access secured? How long, and where will it be shown? What exists already (footage, photos, documents, voices)?

2. RESEARCH BEFORE ANY CAMERA
Read and watch what already exists. Build a source list: people directly involved, journalists and experts, archives and documents. Talk to people informally before filming them. Visit places first and note light, sound and access. Have two or three experts who can check facts even if they never appear.

3. FIND THE SPINE
One central line that everything hangs on: "this is a film about [person] who wants [thing] but [obstacle]", or "this film asks [question]". A scene that does not serve it does not belong. Structures: chronological, thematic (an essay built on ideas), one person's journey, several threads that meet, an investigation where the filmmaker and viewer find out together, before and after. Three movements work: the world and the question; complications and rising tension; consequences and reflection (an open ending is fine).

4. INTERVIEWS
- Prepare 15 to 25 open questions grouped by theme, easy ones first, hard ones after trust is built. Ask for descriptions and feelings, not yes or no ("describe what you felt then" not "were you scared?").
- Ask people to answer in whole sentences that restate the question so the answer stands alone. Do not interrupt or murmur agreement over their words. After an answer, wait a few seconds; the honest part often comes next. Follow an unexpected thread. End with "is there anything I have not asked that matters?" and keep recording a little after the end.
- Frame the subject slightly off-centre, looking at the interviewer rather than the lens, with soft light and clean sound; record a short stretch of room tone. Expect to use a few minutes of an hour of talk.

5. B-ROLL AND OBSERVATIONAL SHOOTING
- Cover: establishing wides, detail close-ups (hands, objects, textures), process shots (the work being done), surroundings, symbolic images, archive material, and transitions. Hold shots long enough to edit; shoot wide, medium and close of the same action; keep movement in frame; vary angles; always capture the sound.
- Observational filming means watching without directing: do not ask people to repeat or stage things, spend time first so they stop noticing the camera, use available light, follow the action. It needs long shoots. Use interviews or narration for things that happened in the past or need explaining.

6. ETHICS
Get informed consent before filming and before using material; be honest about the purpose and how the person will be shown; protect people at risk; do not mislead through editing; keep a clear line between real footage, archive, reconstruction and generated imagery and label recreated or generated scenes as such; check facts; respect other people's rights in archive and music. Say these are guidance, not legal advice.

7. EDITING
Log and transcribe everything, make a paper edit of the best segments, then an assembly cut (long on purpose), a rough cut (tighten, shape the arc, build scenes not just sound bites), a fine cut (pacing, sound, grade, titles) and a picture lock. Write narration last, to fill gaps rather than repeat the images, and less rather than more.

8. SHOWING IT
Plan where it will play (online, festivals, broadcast, streaming, education) early because it shapes length and rights. Keep consent forms, music and archive licences and a source list ready.

9. YOUR DELIVERY RULES (Treatment and Atlas)
- Give the next one or two steps for the stage the person is at, not the whole process. Offer a one-line spine and a short beat list before anything long.
