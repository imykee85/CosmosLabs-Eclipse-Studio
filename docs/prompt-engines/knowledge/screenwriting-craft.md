SCREENWRITING CRAFT

Eclipse notes in our own words, written after reading five screenwriting skills the owner uploaded (premise and theme, story structure, scene craft, dialogue, character and conflict). The originals are kept unchanged in docs/prompt-engines/knowledge/original/screenwriting/. No book passages are quoted here. Use this when the person is writing a story, a script, a scene or dialogue, or shaping a concept; keep it quiet otherwise.

1. PREMISE AND THEME
- A premise is one sentence: someone, in a situation, wants something badly, and something stands in the way. If you cannot say it in one sentence, the idea is not ready.
- The controlling idea is what the story proves by the end: a value and the cause of it ("people lose trust when..."). Every scene should push on it, for or against.
- Ask what is at stake and for whom. No stakes, no story.

2. STRUCTURE
- Three movements: setup, confrontation, resolution. Each ends on a turn that changes what the character must do.
- Key points: the incident that starts it, the first big turn (the point of no return), the middle shift (a win or loss that raises the cost), the lowest point, the climax, and the new normal.
- Each scene or beat must change something. If nothing changes, cut or merge it.
- For short videos, compress: hook is the incident, the middle is the one obstacle, the turn is the reveal, the payoff is the change.

3. CHARACTER AND CONFLICT
- A character has a want (what they chase), a need (what would actually help) and a false belief that keeps them from it. Conflict comes from the gap.
- Opposition must be as strong as the hero, and personal. Conflict has three layers: against others, against the world, against themselves.
- Show character by choices under pressure, not by description.

4. SCENES
- Every scene has a goal, an obstacle, and a turn in value (hope to fear, trust to doubt, safe to danger). Enter late, leave early.
- Start in the middle of the action, end on the change. Write what can be seen and heard; keep thoughts out of the action lines.
- Vary the shape of consecutive scenes so the rhythm moves.

5. DIALOGUE
- Dialogue is action: each line tries to get something from the other person.
- Subtext: people rarely say what they mean. Let the unsaid carry the feeling.
- Cut greetings, explanation and repeating what we can see. Give each speaker a distinct rhythm and word choice. Read it aloud; if it sounds written, rewrite it.
- For video prompts, spoken lines must be short enough for the clip length (about 3 to 4 words a second) and are written as quoted speech.

6. YOUR DELIVERY RULES (Treatment and Atlas)
- Help with the one weak point first (usually the premise or the missing turn), then offer the next.
- Ask at most one short question at a time. When you suggest a rewrite, show it, do not describe it.
